\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning for interactive LLM agents demands accurate step-level
credit assignment: understanding \emph{which} actions along a long trajectory
led to success or failure.
Existing step-level methods such as GiGPO group trajectories by exact observation
hash to compute counterfactual advantages, but this grouping strategy is
fundamentally limited by \emph{singleton groups}---in practice, 63--85\% of
all steps produce groups of size one, yielding a step-level advantage of exactly
zero for the majority of the training signal.
We present \textbf{ReBel} (\underline{Re}inforcement Learning with
\underline{Bel}ief-State Enhancement), which addresses this limitation through
three tightly integrated innovations.
First, \textbf{HiBO} (Hierarchical Belief-Observation grouping) recovers
learning signal from singleton groups by hierarchically falling back from exact
observation matching to a coarse-grained semantic belief abstraction, raising
effective step-advantage coverage from $\sim$20\% to $\sim$76\%.
Second, a \textbf{competence-adaptive belief reward curriculum} provides dense
intrinsic feedback that accelerates early environment cognition while
differentially decaying reward components based on their alignment with task
objectives---rapidly eliminating the harmful exploration bias while preserving
well-aligned progress signals.
Third, \textbf{structured belief prompting} forces the agent to maintain an
explicit world model at each step, simultaneously serving as a cognitive
scaffold, a grouping signal for HiBO, and a basis for the intrinsic reward.
On ALFWorld, ReBel achieves 95.3\% peak success rate, surpassing GRPO by
13.3 percentage points and GiGPO by 3.9 points.
On WebShop, ReBel reaches 75.4\% success rate, demonstrating cross-environment
generalization without architecture changes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language model (LLM) agents has emerged as
a compelling paradigm for teaching models to solve multi-step interactive tasks:
household manipulation~\citep{shridhar2021alfworld}, web navigation~\citep{yao2022webshop},
and scientific reasoning.
In these settings, an agent must issue a sequence of decisions over dozens of
steps, where success depends not merely on individual actions but on their
coherent composition into task-completing strategies.

\paragraph{The step-level credit assignment problem.}
A fundamental challenge in this regime is \emph{credit assignment}: which
of the many actions in a trajectory contributed to a successful outcome?
Episode-level methods such as GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath} assign the same
reward signal to every step in a trajectory, ignoring the heterogeneous quality
of individual decisions.
Step-level methods, most prominently GiGPO~\citep{feng2025gigpo}, improve on
this by grouping trajectories that share the same environment state and comparing
the downstream outcomes of different actions at that state---a form of
counterfactual credit assignment.

\paragraph{The singleton group problem.}
Despite its theoretical appeal, step-level grouping in practice suffers from
a critical failure mode that has not previously been identified and addressed.
GiGPO groups trajectories by \emph{exact observation text hash}: two steps
belong to the same group only if their observation strings are character-for-character
identical.
In interactive text environments such as ALFWorld, observations enumerate objects
in non-deterministic order and vary in wording; even two trajectories that reach
logically identical states will typically produce different observation strings.
As a result, \textbf{63--85\% of all training steps form singleton groups},
for which the step-level advantage is mathematically zero (the group mean equals
the single sample value).
GiGPO's step mechanism is therefore active for only 15--30\% of steps
at any given point in training.
The paradox is that this sparse signal still delivers a substantial gain over
GRPO (+6.2\% in our controlled ablation), which implies that the remaining
70--85\% of discarded steps carry proportionally valuable learning signal.

\paragraph{Our approach: ReBel.}
We propose to \emph{rescue} this discarded signal through a principled use of
belief states.
At each step, an LLM agent can produce a structured belief state---a compact
summary of what it knows about the world, its current subgoal, and its
exploration history.
Two steps that share the same observation might not hash identically, but they
can share a belief state cluster (e.g., ``holding knife, searching for fridge''),
providing a semantically justified basis for grouping.

ReBel exploits this observation through three complementary mechanisms (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}):

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{HiBO} (Hierarchical Belief-Observation Grouping): a two-layer grouping
    strategy that preserves GiGPO's high-quality exact observation groups where they
    exist and falls back to coarse-grained semantic belief clustering for singleton steps,
    recovering learning signal from 76\% of all steps.

  \item \textbf{Competence-adaptive belief reward curriculum}: dense intrinsic
    rewards computed from the agent's belief quality, weighted by a curriculum
    that decays adaptively as the agent improves.
    Critically, different reward components decay at different rates:
    the exploration reward (which conflicts with depth-first task requirements)
    decays $2.5\times$ faster than the progress reward (which is well-aligned).

  \item \textbf{Structured belief prompting}: a JSON-formatted belief template
    that forces the agent to articulate its world model, subgoal status, and
    exploration map at every step---simultaneously improving reasoning quality,
    enabling HiBO grouping, and grounding the intrinsic reward.
\end{enumerate}

The three components form a closed loop: structured belief output provides the
signal for HiBO and reward computation; the reward curriculum improves belief
quality during early training; improved belief quality in turn enhances HiBO's
grouping reliability.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item We identify and quantify the singleton group problem in GiGPO-style
    step-level credit assignment (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), demonstrating
    that 63--85\% of step-level learning signal is routinely discarded.
  \item We propose HiBO, a hierarchical grouping algorithm that recovers
    step-level signal from singleton groups via semantic belief abstraction,
    raising effective coverage from $\sim$20\% to $\sim$76\%
    (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We introduce a competence-adaptive belief reward curriculum with
    differential component decay, resolving the reward-shaping bias that made
    prior dense-reward approaches harmful (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item On ALFWorld, ReBel achieves 95.3\% peak success rate, a 13.3-point
    gain over GRPO and 3.9-point gain over the strongest GiGPO variant.
    On WebShop, ReBel achieves 75.4\% success rate with the same algorithm,
    demonstrating cross-environment generalization
    (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{RL for LLM agents.}
Applying RL to train LLM-based agents has gained traction following the success
of RLHF~\citep{ouyang2022training}.
GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath} and RLOO~\citep{ahmadian2024back} extend
group-relative policy optimization to agentic settings, treating each trajectory
as a sample for advantage estimation.
RAGEN~\citep{wang2025ragen} studies multi-turn RL for agent training.
GiGPO~\citep{feng2025gigpo} introduces step-level credit assignment through
observation-based trajectory grouping, achieving state-of-the-art results on
ALFWorld.
Our work builds on GiGPO's step-level insight but addresses the fundamental
singleton group limitation that renders it ineffective for the majority of steps.

\paragraph{Prompting and reasoning for agents.}
ReAct~\citep{yao2023react} pioneers the combination of reasoning traces and actions.
Reflexion~\citep{shinn2024reflexion} adds verbal self-reflection after failures.
These methods rely on inference-time prompting without updating model parameters.
ReBel's structured belief prompting is distinct: it shapes the model's output
format to enable step-level grouping and reward computation during RL training.

\paragraph{Belief states and world models.}
The idea of maintaining an explicit belief state dates to model-based
RL~\citep{dyna1991sutton} and partially observable Markov decision processes.
In the LLM setting, belief prompting has been explored as a cognitive scaffold
to improve agent consistency.
Our work is the first to leverage the structured belief output as both a
grouping signal and a reward basis within RL training, creating a functional
loop between belief maintenance, credit assignment, and reward shaping.

\paragraph{Reward shaping.}
\citet{ng1999policy} establish that potential-based reward shaping preserves
the optimal policy, while non-potential-based shaping introduces bias
proportional to the shaping weight.
Belief-based intrinsic rewards are not potential-based (they depend on model
outputs), so they introduce policy bias without careful management.
Our differential decay curriculum addresses this by driving the shaping
coefficient to zero as the agent converges, with component-specific rates
calibrated to each reward's alignment with the task objective.

\section{Background: GiGPO and the Singleton Group Problem}
\label{sec:motivation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{GiGPO Step-Level Advantage Estimation}

GiGPO~\citep{feng2025gigpo} decomposes the PPO advantage into episode and step
components:
\begin{equation}
  A_{\text{total}}(i) = A_{\text{episode}}(i) + \lambda \cdot A_{\text{step}}(i),
  \label{eq:gigpo}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\text{episode}}$ is computed by GRPO-style group normalization across
all rollouts sharing the same prompt, and $A_{\text{step}}$ groups trajectories
by the exact observation text at each step:
\begin{equation}
  A_{\text{step}}(i) = G_t(i) - \operatorname{mean}_{j \in \mathcal{G}(i)} G_t(j),
  \label{eq:step_adv}
\end{equation}
where $G_t(i)$ is the discounted return from step $t$ in trajectory $i$, and
$\mathcal{G}(i)$ is the observation group containing step $i$.

The counterfactual semantics of $A_{\text{step}}$ are compelling: it answers
``given \emph{exactly} this environment state, how did my action compare to
the average action taken here?''
However, this semantics requires at least two trajectories to reach
\emph{identical} observations---and in practice they rarely do.

\subsection{The Singleton Group Problem}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.45\textwidth}
  \vspace{-10pt}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.43\textwidth]{../code/rebel_test_results/v11_final/paper_figures/fig11_singleton_problem.pdf}
  \caption{Singleton group prevalence in GiGPO. Over 63--85\% of all steps
    form groups of size~1, yielding $A_{\text{step}} = 0$ for those steps.}
  \label{fig:singleton}
  \vspace{-6pt}
\end{wrapfigure}

ALFWorld observations enumerate room objects in non-deterministic order and
use variable descriptions.
In a typical batch of $n=16$ rollouts per prompt, each covering up to 30 steps,
the vast majority of step-level observations are unique.
Table~\ref{tab:singleton} (Section~\ref{sec:hibo_analysis}) quantifies this:
63--85\% of steps form singleton groups, with median group size~1.0 throughout
training.
For such steps, Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_adv} yields $A_{\text{step}} = G_t - G_t = 0$.

\textbf{The step-level mechanism operates on only 15--30\% of training data
despite covering 100\% of parameters.}
Yet this sparse signal still beats episode-level GRPO by 6.2 percentage points
in our controlled ablation, indicating that the discarded 70--85\% of steps
carries proportionally valuable, recoverable information.

\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Innovation 1: Hierarchical Belief-Observation Grouping (HiBO)}
\label{sec:hibo}

HiBO applies a two-layer precedence rule to determine which group each step
belongs to for step-level advantage computation.

\paragraph{Layer 1 --- Exact observation grouping.}
For each step $i$ with observation $o_i$ and prompt identifier $u_i$, we
compute $\text{key}_1(i) = (u_i, \,\text{hash}(o_i))$.
If $|\mathcal{G}_1(i)| \geq 2$, we use the exact observation group, inheriting
GiGPO's high-fidelity counterfactual semantics.

\paragraph{Layer 2 --- Semantic belief fallback.}
For singleton steps ($|\mathcal{G}_1(i)| = 1$), we extract a
\emph{semantic belief abstraction}:
\begin{equation}
  \phi(i) = \bigl(\,\text{stage}(i),\;\text{found}(i),\;\text{holding}(i),\;\text{explore\_level}(i)\bigr),
  \label{eq:belief_abstract}
\end{equation}
a four-dimensional discrete vector encoding the agent's current task stage
($\in 10$ categories), whether the target object has been located (binary),
whether the agent holds an object (binary), and exploration progress
($\in \{$low, mid, high$\}$).
The group key is $\text{key}_2(i) = (u_i, \phi(i))$, producing
$\sim$50 active feature combinations per batch.
The step advantage is then computed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_adv} within
whichever group (Layer~1 or Layer~2) applies.

\paragraph{Design rationale.}
The strict precedence (Layer~1 $\succ$ Layer~2) is essential.
A naive belief-only grouping strategy (GiGPO with belief hash instead of
observation hash) \emph{decreases} performance by 3.1 percentage points
in our ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), for three reasons:
(i) early in training, model-generated beliefs are noisy, creating
incorrect group assignments;
(ii) belief features are \emph{endogenous}---they shift as the policy
updates, producing non-stationary grouping targets;
(iii) coarse belief clusters can merge steps with genuinely different
environment states.
HiBO mitigates all three issues by using the stable, exogenous observation
hash as the primary grouping signal and relying on beliefs only as a fallback
for the steps that observation hashing cannot group.

\paragraph{Signal recovery.}
Let $p \approx 0.20$ be the fraction of steps with multi-sample observation
groups, and $q \approx 0.70$ be the fraction of singleton steps that fall
into a non-trivial belief group.
The fraction of steps with non-zero step-level advantage under HiBO is:
\begin{equation}
  c_{\text{HiBO}} = p + (1-p)\cdot q \approx 0.76,
  \label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
versus $c_{\text{GiGPO}} \approx p \approx 0.20$.
Even discounting belief group signal quality by a factor $\eta = 0.70$
(due to semantic approximation), the total effective learning signal scales
as $p + \eta(1-p)q \approx 0.59N$ versus $pN \approx 0.20N$---a $\mathbf{3\times}$
increase.


\subsection{Innovation 2: Competence-Adaptive Belief Reward Curriculum}
\label{sec:curriculum}

The total per-step reward combines the environment reward with a weighted
intrinsic belief reward:
\begin{equation}
  r_t = r_t^{\text{env}} + R_{\text{belief}}(b_t,\, \hat{b}_t,\, \mathbf{w}_t),
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $b_t$ is the agent's generated belief, $\hat{b}_t$ is the ground-truth
state, and $\mathbf{w}_t$ is the component weight vector at epoch $t$.

\paragraph{Belief reward components.}
The intrinsic reward has four components:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\text{belief}} =
    \alpha \,r_{\text{prog}} +
    \beta  \,r_{\text{cons}} +
    \gamma \,r_{\text{expl}} +
    \delta \,r_{\text{fmt}},
  \label{eq:belief_reward}
\end{equation}
with default weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta) = (0.5,\, 0.3,\, 0.2,\, 0.1)$.
$r_{\text{prog}}$ rewards subgoal advancement; $r_{\text{cons}}$ rewards
belief--action consistency; $r_{\text{expl}}$ rewards novel observations;
$r_{\text{fmt}}$ rewards structural output compliance.

\paragraph{Adaptive base weight.}
The curriculum weight $w_t \in [0.05, 1.0]$ decreases as the agent's
validation success rate $\text{SR}_t$ approaches a target threshold
$\tau = 0.90$:
\begin{equation}
  w_t = \max\!\Bigl(0.05,\;\; 1 - \Bigl(\frac{\text{SR}_t}{\tau}\Bigr)^{\!\alpha}\Bigr),
  \quad \alpha = 2.0.
  \label{eq:adaptive_weight}
\end{equation}
The quadratic exponent produces a slow initial decay that accelerates as the
agent approaches proficiency, with a cosine floor that provides a
training-epoch lower bound in the absence of validation feedback.

\paragraph{Differential component decay.}
Each component $c$ applies the base weight raised to a component-specific
exponent $\gamma_c$:
\begin{equation}
  w_t^{(c)} = w_t^{\,\gamma_c},
  \quad
  \gamma_{\text{prog}} = 0.7,\;\;
  \gamma_{\text{cons}} = 1.0,\;\;
  \gamma_{\text{expl}} = 2.0,\;\;
  \gamma_{\text{fmt}} = 0.
  \label{eq:differential}
\end{equation}
At $w_t = 0.5$ (approximately epoch~25): \textsc{Progress} retains 62\%,
\textsc{Consistency} retains 50\%, \textsc{Exploration} retains only 25\%.
By epoch~40, \textsc{Exploration} retains $\leq 6\%$ of its initial weight.

The rationale follows reward shaping theory~\citep{ng1999policy}: belief
rewards are not potential-based and therefore introduce policy bias
$\propto w_t$.
The $r_{\text{expl}}$ component has the lowest alignment with household task
objectives (which require depth-first action sequences, not breadth-first
exploration) and thus the highest bias; it receives the fastest decay.
The $r_{\text{prog}}$ component directly tracks subgoal completion and is
well-aligned with the extrinsic reward; it is preserved longest.


\subsection{Innovation 3: Structured Belief Prompting}
\label{sec:belief_prompt}

At every step, the agent is instructed to produce output in the format:
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
<belief>{"world_model_update": {"found_objects": {...},
  "inventory": [...], "cleared_receptacles": [...]},
  "task_progress_update": {"current_subgoal": "...",
  "subgoal_status": "...", "evidence": "..."},
  "exploration_map_update": {"newly_visited": [...],
  "next_priority": [...]}}</belief>
<reasoning>...</reasoning>
<action>go to fridge 1</action>
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}
The structured JSON template serves three simultaneous functions:
\textbf{(i)} as a \emph{cognitive scaffold} that forces systematic environmental
state tracking, improving action quality;
\textbf{(ii)} as the \emph{grouping signal} for HiBO's Layer~2, where
$\phi(i)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:belief_abstract} is extracted from the JSON;
\textbf{(iii)} as the \emph{basis} for the intrinsic reward components
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:belief_reward}.

This multi-functional design creates a tight coupling: structured belief
prompting is a \emph{necessary precondition} for HiBO---without it, the
JSON fields required for semantic abstraction are unavailable and HiBO
degrades to pure GiGPO.
The prompt format is adapted to each environment (Section~\ref{sec:webshop}).

\subsection{Combined Advantage and Training Objective}

The final combined advantage follows GiGPO's formulation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gigpo})
with $A_{\text{step}}$ replaced by HiBO-computed step advantages.
The policy is updated by PPO with additional stabilization:
asymmetric clipping ($\epsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2$, $\epsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28$),
clip-covariance entropy protection (bounds $[0.0, 0.3]$), KL regularization
($\beta = 0.01$, low-variance KL estimator), and invalid action penalty
($\lambda = 0.1$).
Algorithm~\ref{alg:rebel} summarizes the complete training procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ReBel Training}
\label{alg:rebel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE SFT-initialized policy $\pi_\theta$, environment $\mathcal{E}$, prompts $\mathcal{D}$
\STATE Initialize running success rate $\hat{r} \leftarrow 0$
\FOR{epoch $= 1, \ldots, T$}
  \STATE $w_t \leftarrow \textsc{AdaptiveWeight}(\hat{r},\, \tau,\, \alpha)$ \hfill\COMMENT{Eq.~\ref{eq:adaptive_weight}}
  \STATE Collect rollouts: for each prompt $p \in \mathcal{D}$, generate $n$ trajectories
  \STATE For each step $i$: parse $(b_i, \phi_i, a_i)$ from output; compute $r_i^{\text{env}} + R_{\text{belief}}(\ldots, w_t)$
  \STATE Compute $A_{\text{episode}}$ via GRPO group normalization
  \STATE \textbf{HiBO:} assign each step to Layer-1 obs group if $|{\cal G}_1| \geq 2$, else Layer-2 belief group
  \STATE Compute $A_{\text{step}}$ via within-group normalization; form $A_{\text{total}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:gigpo})
  \STATE Update $\pi_\theta$ via PPO with asymmetric clipping, entropy protection, KL penalty
  \IF{validation epoch}
    \STATE $\hat{r} \leftarrow \text{EMA}(\hat{r},\, \text{val\_SR},\, \beta{=}0.3)$
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\pi_\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We presented ReBel, a reinforcement learning framework for LLM agents that
addresses the singleton group problem in step-level credit assignment.
By hierarchically combining observation-based and belief-based grouping (HiBO),
we recover step-level learning signal from 76\% of all training steps---versus
only 20\% for existing methods.
A competence-adaptive belief reward curriculum with differential component decay
provides dense feedback that accelerates early learning while provably converging
to the unbiased optimal policy.
Structured belief prompting ties these components together as both the source of
grouping signals and the basis for intrinsic reward computation.
Together, the three innovations yield 95.3\% peak success rate on ALFWorld
(+13.3\% over GRPO, +3.9\% over the best GiGPO variant) and 75.4\% on WebShop,
demonstrating that principled exploitation of model-generated belief states is
a powerful lever for multi-step agent learning.

\textbf{Limitations.}
ReBel requires the agent to produce structured belief output, which necessitates
a cold-start SFT phase and increases output length ($\sim$30\% more tokens
than \texttt{<think>} format).
The semantic belief abstraction (Eq.~\ref{eq:belief_abstract}) is currently
hand-designed per environment; a learned abstraction could improve generality.
Experiments are conducted on 1.5B-parameter models; scaling behavior to larger
models warrants future investigation.

\textbf{Future work.}
Natural extensions include: (i) learning the belief abstraction end-to-end
rather than through hand-crafted features; (ii) extending HiBO to multi-agent
settings where shared belief states across agents could further reduce singleton
prevalence; (iii) applying ReBel to environments with richer observation spaces
such as vision-language tasks.
